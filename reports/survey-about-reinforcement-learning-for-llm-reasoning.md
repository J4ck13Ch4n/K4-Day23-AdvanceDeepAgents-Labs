# Survey about Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning

## TL;DR
- Reinforcement learning (RL) improves reasoning abilities in large language models (LLMs) by optimizing reward functions, policy selection, and adaptive reasoning strategies [1][2].
- State-of-the-art RL methods enable parameter-efficient LLM fine-tuning, cross-domain benchmarking, competence-difficulty alignment, and selective rollout filtering, significantly enhancing reasoning performance [3][4][5][6].
- RL-driven reasoning is applied in domains such as mathematics, medicine, and vision-language tasks, but evaluation bottlenecks, dataset creation costs, and generalization challenges persist [7][8][9][10].
- RL for LLMs faces obstacles like hallucination, reward model bias, and the credit assignment problem when scaling to diverse real-world reasoning tasks [9][10].
- Emerging benchmarks and frameworks are addressing these issues by evolving tests for adaptability, robustness, and clinical relevance [11][12][13][9][10].

## Background
Reinforcement learning for large language models focuses on designing reward functions, optimizing policy updates, and sampling strategies to enhance reasoning abilities [2]. Key approaches include RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), verifiable reward systems (using correctness checks or unit tests), and dense or unsupervised rewards. Entropy collapse in RL optimization can limit exploration and reasoning diversity, but improved rollout selection techniques mitigate these weaknesses [14]. Adaptive reasoning, such as cross-turn estimation, helps reduce unnecessary computation and focuses reasoning steps where most effective [1]. Foundational surveys highlight the need for scalable RL methods, balanced reward design, and integration of RL with supervised fine-tuning.

## Foundational Approaches: Reward Design and Adaptive Strategies
RL for LLM reasoning is fundamentally shaped by how rewards are defined and assigned. Verifiable rewards, based on correctness assessments, explicit answer verification, or unit tests, provide robust benchmarks for reasoning [2][8][9]. Policy optimization and rollout selection strategies such as GRPO mitigate entropy collapse—an RL-specific weakness that reduces reasoning diversity—by refining which rollouts are used for policy updates, not only by regularization but by filtering for the most informative samples [14]. Selective rollout filtering and adaptive mechanisms, such as cross-turn estimation, further improve efficiency and accuracy by only requiring deep reasoning when the context demands it, rather than at every interaction step [1][6].

RLHF is a dominant approach for LLMs: it leverages human preference rankings or feedback to train reward models that guide LLM optimization for reasoning tasks [2]. Dense and unsupervised reward systems have also been explored, providing broader coverage for situations where explicit feedback isn’t feasible. Surveys and foundational works emphasize major ongoing challenges: scaling RL algorithms and data pipelines to large models, defining reliable reward functions, and integrating RL with supervised fine-tuning for versatile LLM reasoning [2][7]. Reward specification remains a key bottleneck—balancing accuracy, interpretability, and cost is crucial for effective generalization and deployment.

## Advancements: Efficient Training, Domain Adaptation, and Benchmarking
Recent advances focus heavily on training efficiency and resource optimization. S-GRPO and T-SPMO are new RL algorithms designed for fine-tuning LLMs with strong accuracy even under limited memory and compute budgets, making practical RL-driven reasoning improvements accessible for a wider variety of LLM deployments [3]. To evaluate reasoning across knowledge boundaries, the Guru corpus brings diverse, cross-domain RL benchmarks into play, confirming that domain-specific RL training produces significant performance improvements, especially on complex reasoning tasks [4].

Competence-difficulty alignment, exemplified by CDAS sampling, enables LLMs to learn from tasks that match their current skill—resulting in higher efficiency and accuracy on challenging reasoning benchmarks, such as those focused on mathematics [5]. Selective rollout algorithms like GRESO further boost computational and training efficiency by reducing unnecessary updates, maintaining solid performance [6]. On the large-scale and systems side, MiMo-V2.6 introduces a hybrid-SWA architecture and large-scale asynchronous training, enabling foundation models to continually improve through RL on multimodal corpora and dynamic batch scaling [12]. Frameworks like Memento 3 highlight the potential of recursive learning and explicit world-model development: frozen LLMs can update semantic rulebooks and integrate new environment knowledge, supporting continual adaptation and self-improvement—even in environments never seen during training [13].

## Applications: Reasoning in Mathematics, Medicine, and Multimodal Tasks
Applications of RL for LLM reasoning are most visible in data-rich, high-stakes domains. Process-level reward models (PRMs) are increasingly favored in fields such as mathematics, legal analysis, and medicine, as they distribute rewards to intermediate reasoning steps and align better with human step-by-step preferences. This leads to performance gains on specialized benchmarks like GSM8K for math or medical QA suites [7][9].

Medical reasoning particularly benefits from standardization: Medmarks, an open-source benchmark suite, provides 30 tests for question answering and clinical reasoning, showcasing RL's capability to boost accuracy, though bottlenecks persist due to inaccessible or saturated datasets and dataset creation costs [9]. The intersection of RL and visual-language models is marked by RL methods leveraging verifiable rewards for tasks requiring both reasoning and vision (e.g., diagnosis from x-rays), yet weakness in modeling visual features during RL training hampers their generalization [8]. In unfamiliar or dynamic environments, platforms such as Learn2Play Bench allow RL-guided LLMs to distinguish between learning from new experience and relying on pretrained reasoning, testing adaptability and world-model acquisition [11]. However, evaluation remains dependent on benchmark availability, with high annotation costs and the inherent difficulty of assessing step-level correctness limiting practical impact and broad adoption [7][9].

## Limitations: Scaling, Hallucination, and Evaluation Challenges
Limitations of RL-driven LLM reasoning are significant, especially for large-scale and real-world use. Hallucination risk remains high for RL-trained LLMs, particularly in medical, legal, or safety-critical applications, where failure to reliably verify outputs can produce dangerous errors [10]. Static benchmarking methods, despite improvements, tend to saturate quickly and do not test long-term or evolving robustness. Biases in reward modeling and the credit assignment problem make it difficult to proportion rewards fairly across complex reasoning chains, influencing both training and evaluation outcomes [7].

Step-level evaluation, essential for task decomposition and true reasoning progress, is hindered by expensive annotation costs and limited protocol coverage, particularly those ensuring clinical relevance and practical applicability [9]. Integration bottlenecks are also apparent in multimodal models: vision-language LLMs lack explicit, effective fusion of visual representation learning and RL optimization, which reduces generalization outside of narrow tasks [8][10]. Finally, open-source, comprehensive benchmark datasets—critical for both evaluation and further research—remain scarce, creating persistent accessibility challenges [9].

## Trends and Open Problems
Trends in RL for LLM reasoning target scalable, efficient training, and increased reliability in practical deployment. Architectures such as MiMo-V2.6 are pushing the limits of RL compute infrastructure, enabling asynchronous, large-batch training and continual self-improvement in LLMs [12]. The emergence of process-level reward models has directly improved reasoning alignment with human stepwise judgments, boosting performance in both complex and real-world tasks [7][9].

Adaptability and robustness are targeted by frameworks like Unified Hallucination Fuzzing, which brings evolving stress tests into benchmarking toolkits, yet there remains a critical need for benchmarks that adapt to new tasks and unpredictably evolving requirements [10]. Major open problems include reducing the cost and effort of data annotation, building transferable RL strategies that generalize across domains, and designing reward mechanisms that reliably translate improved reasoning quality into safer, more interpretable real-world outcomes [7][9][10]. Attention is also growing around the fusion of RL with multimodal representations (text, vision), external memory systems, and recursive rule-based environments, alongside the development of open-source benchmarks with broader practical coverage [11][8][9].
